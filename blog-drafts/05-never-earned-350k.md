# Never earned more than $350,000, worth nearly $10 million at 52

> **In short:** A 52-year-old shared on r/fatFIRE that they never earned more than $350,000 a year, yet built a net worth of nearly $10 million. The secrets: equity compensation from the companies they worked for, steady saving into retirement accounts, bouncing back quickly after layoffs, and owning everything outright.

## The starting point

A good salary, yes, but not the kind of income that usually builds a fortune on its own. They never earned more than $350,000 a year, which is well paid but far from the multimillion-dollar packages behind many Fat FIRE stories.

## The turning point

Several of the companies they worked for offered equity-based compensation: stock and incentive plans on top of salary. Instead of treating that as spending money, they let it build wealth.

## The plan

- **Take advantage of equity compensation** when employers offered it.
- **Fill up the retirement accounts.** Over the years, a 401(k) and a self-directed IRA grew into millions.
- **Build a taxable investment account** on top of that, managed with a certified financial planner.
- **Own everything outright.** The home and all five cars are paid off.
- **Bounce back fast.** They went through layoffs, but found new work quickly each time.

## The hard parts

Layoffs. Several times, the job that funded everything disappeared. What saved them was finding new work quickly, and having no debt, so a gap in income was never a crisis. Equity compensation also comes with risk: if too much of your wealth sits in your employer's stock, a bad year for the company can hit your job and your savings at the same time.

## Where they are now

At 52, with nearly $10 million and no debt, work is entirely optional. Even a very cautious withdrawal rate would fund a generous lifestyle for the rest of their life.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **Equity comp is part of your pay, so invest it like part of your pay.** Don't spend it as a bonus.
- **No debt makes layoffs survivable.** A paid-off house and cars turn a job loss into an inconvenience instead of a disaster.
- **Retirement accounts compound quietly.** More than $4.5 million of their wealth sits in a 401(k) and an IRA.

## How to do this in the US

**Equity compensation comes in several forms**, each with its own tax rules. RSUs are taxed as ordinary income when they vest. With an employee stock purchase plan (ESPP), you buy company stock at a discount. Stock options have their own timing and tax rules. A common FIRE habit is to sell RSUs as they vest and reinvest in a diversified index fund, so you're not betting both your job and your savings on one company.

**A self-directed IRA** lets you hold a wider range of investments than a standard IRA, but it comes with strict rules and higher fees. For most people, a regular IRA invested in low-cost index funds is simpler.

**High earners can still use a Roth.** If your income is too high to contribute to a Roth IRA directly, the "backdoor Roth" (contributing to a traditional IRA and converting it) is a widely used and legal route. Check the details with a tax professional.
