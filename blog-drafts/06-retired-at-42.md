## The starting point

Like most people who reach FI in their early forties, this poster spent their working years saving and investing heavily. What makes their story useful isn't how they got there, but how their money is arranged now that they're there.

## The turning point

At 42, with $3 million invested and a paid-off property, they decided they had enough. The question was no longer "how much", but "from which account, and when?"

## The plan

Their $3 million sits in three very different kinds of account:

- **About $1.3 million in a taxable brokerage account.** No age limits at all, so this is the money they can live on first.
- **About $1.2 million in a 401(k) and other retirement accounts.** Locked behind a 10% penalty before 59½, with some important exceptions.
- **About $500,000 in a Roth IRA.** Contributions can be withdrawn at any time. Earnings have to wait.

On top of that, they own a **$500,000 one-bedroom condo outright**.

## The hard parts

A 42-year-old's retirement may need to last 50 years, longer than the 30 years the classic 4% rule was built on. That's why a slightly lower withdrawal rate, like 3.7%, makes sense. Owning property also comes with costs that are easy to underestimate: property taxes, insurance, HOA fees and maintenance can eat a big share of the rent (or the savings) a condo brings in.

## Life after FI

With about $111,000 a year available at a cautious withdrawal rate, a paid-off home and a well-balanced mix of accounts, they're in a strong position to enjoy decades of freedom.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **Build a taxable account, not just retirement accounts.** It's the bridge that makes retiring in your forties possible.
- **Use a lower withdrawal rate for a longer retirement.** 3.5–3.75% gives a 50-year retirement more breathing room.
- **Paid-off property brings security, and costs.** Budget for taxes, insurance and repairs.

## How to do this in the US

The standard **"bucket" order** for early retirees looks like this:

1. **Taxable brokerage account first.** You only pay tax on the gains you realize, and long-term gains can be taxed at 0% up to a certain income level.
2. **Roth IRA contributions next.** What you put *in* (not the growth) can come out at any time, tax- and penalty-free.
3. **401(k) and traditional IRA money**, reached before 59½ through:
   - a **Roth conversion ladder** (convert a slice each year, then withdraw it penalty-free after five years),
   - **72(t) SEPP** (substantially equal periodic payments, a fixed withdrawal schedule the IRS allows at any age), or
   - the **Rule of 55**, if you leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 (for that employer's plan).
4. **Everything is fully open at 59½.** Social Security can start from 62.

Getting the order right can save tens of thousands in taxes. Try different spending levels in the [years-to-FI calculator](index.html#calc-years).
