# She retired at 35 while her husband kept working: the "one-spouse FIRE" plan

> **In short:** A woman in her mid-thirties with a net worth of about $2.5 million, around $1.5 million of it invested, left a job she found miserable. Her husband is keeping his job, supports her decision, and his salary covers most of their expenses. They expect to draw only $1,000 to $2,000 a month from savings.

## The starting point

By her mid-thirties, she and her husband had built a net worth of about $2.5 million. But she was in a job that made her miserable, and the money they'd built gave her a choice most people don't have.

## The turning point

The job itself was the trigger. Once she saw that their finances could handle it, staying in a job that made her unhappy no longer made sense.

## The plan

- **She stops working.** Her husband keeps his job, and fully supports her decision.
- **His salary covers most of the bills.** That means their investments barely need to be touched.
- **Draw a little from savings.** They expect to take out about $1,000–$2,000 a month to cover the gap.
- **Let the portfolio keep growing.** Taking out only $12,000–$24,000 a year from about $1.5 million invested is a withdrawal rate of roughly 1–1.6%, so the portfolio is likely to keep growing.

## The hard parts

The articles covering her story focused less on money and more on meaning. She needs to find something to fill her days and give her a sense of purpose and satisfaction outside work. That's often the hardest part of leaving a career early. One-spouse FIRE also depends on the working spouse staying happy with the arrangement. Open, ongoing conversation matters, because if one partner starts to feel they're carrying the household, resentment can build.

## Life after FI

She's free of the job that made her miserable, with a portfolio that will likely keep growing and a husband who's on board. The next chapter is figuring out what she *wants* to do with her time.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **FI doesn't have to be all or nothing.** One partner can stop while the other keeps working, and it can transform a family's quality of life.
- **A low withdrawal rate gives you options.** With a working spouse, the portfolio keeps growing, which can mean the second retirement comes sooner.
- **Plan the purpose, not just the money.** Know what you're retiring *to*.

## How to do this in the US

**A working spouse solves the biggest problem: health insurance.** Coverage through a spouse's employer plan avoids the cost and complexity of the ACA marketplace before Medicare at 65.

**Keep saving for the non-working spouse.** A **spousal IRA** lets a working spouse contribute to an IRA (traditional or Roth) for a non-working spouse, as long as the couple files jointly and has enough earned income. It's an easy way to keep growing retirement savings for both of you.

**Social Security has spousal benefits.** A spouse with few or no earnings can receive up to half of the working spouse's full benefit (at full retirement age), so stepping back from work early hurts less than you might think.
