# From rural China to retired at 31: the couple who refused to buy a house

> **In short:** Kristy Shen grew up poor in rural China. She and her partner Bryce Leung became software engineers in Toronto, skipped the expensive house everyone told them to buy, invested the difference, and retired with about $1 million when she was 31 and he was 32. They've travelled the world on about $40,000 a year ever since.

## The starting point

Kristy Shen was born and raised in a poor family in rural China, where money was always scarce. That shaped everything that came after. When she later built a career as a computer engineer in Canada, she knew exactly what security was worth, and how much she didn't want to depend on a paycheck.

## The turning point

In Toronto, the expected next step for a young professional couple was to buy a house, in one of the most expensive housing markets in the world. They ran the numbers and decided not to. In Shen's words: *"It was all because we didn't buy a house."* The money that would have gone into a mortgage, property tax and upkeep went into investments instead.

## The plan

- **Rent instead of buying,** and invest the difference.
- **Keep spending low** while earning two engineering salaries.
- **Build a portfolio that pays the bills.** They used what they call a *yield shield*: tilting part of the portfolio towards higher-paying dividend and interest investments, raising its yield from about 2% to about 3.5%. On $1 million, that's roughly $35,000 a year in payouts without selling anything.
- **Keep a small cash cushion.** Instead of holding years of living costs in cash, they only cover the gap between their payouts and their spending. A $5,000 yearly gap times five years means about $25,000 in cash, not $200,000.

## The hard parts

Saying no to a house meant going against family, friends and the whole culture around them, especially in Toronto, where house prices only seemed to go up. It also meant living with the fear of missing out. Their bet was that freedom was worth more than home ownership, and for them it paid off.

## Life after FI

They've travelled the world for years, mixing expensive countries (they mention the UK and Denmark) with cheap ones like Thailand and Poland, so the average stays around $40,000 a year. They also write the *Millennial Revolution* blog and wrote *Quit Like a Millionaire*.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **Question the "normal" big purchases.** A house can be a great choice, but it isn't automatically the right one.
- **Make your portfolio pay you.** A slightly higher-yielding mix and a small cash cushion can soften a market crash.
- **Geoarbitrage works.** Living part of the year somewhere cheaper stretches your money a long way.

## What it would look like in Denmark

Their roughly 40,000 Canadian dollars a year is about **190,000 kr.**, which gives an FI number of about **4.7 million kr.** with the 4% rule, around **15,800 kr. a month**.

The house question is just as relevant in Copenhagen and Aarhus, where prices are high. Renting and investing the difference can work here too, though it depends heavily on rent levels and interest rates. One Danish detail: dividends and gains outside pension accounts are taxed as *aktieindkomst* (27% up to a yearly threshold, 42% above it). An *aktiesparekonto*, taxed at a flat 17% but with a cap on how much you can put in, is a good first stop.
