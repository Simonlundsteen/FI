# Retired at 33 with three kids: the engineer who went from zero to a million in ten years

> **In short:** Justin McCurry started his career as a civil engineer on a modest salary, saved at least half of what he and his wife earned, and retired at 33 to be a stay-at-home dad to their three children. The family has lived on a budget of roughly $24,000–$40,000 a year ever since, depending on the year.

## The starting point

Justin McCurry, who writes the blog *Root of Good*, didn't start with a big salary. His first engineering jobs paid between $24,000 and $34,000 a year. He and his wife started their working lives with close to nothing in the bank.

## The turning point

From the very beginning, they decided to save before spending. As soon as they started working, they put the maximum allowed into their tax-advantaged retirement accounts (401(k)s and IRAs). That habit, started on day one, did more than any single big decision later.

## The plan

In his own words, two things mattered most:

1. **A savings rate of 50% or more.** He calls it the biggest single factor by far.
2. **Low-cost, tax-efficient investing.** Cheap index funds, held in the most tax-friendly accounts possible.

Around that, they kept their costs low rather than letting their lifestyle grow with their income.

## The hard parts

Retiring with three children means planning for costs that most single early retirees never think about, from growing grocery bills to college and, in the US, health insurance before government coverage starts. Their budget has grown over the years as the kids got older. That's normal, and it's a good reminder to build some room into any FI plan.

## Life after FI

McCurry describes himself as a former civil engineer turned hard-working stay-at-home dad. On the blog he shares regular early retirement updates, including family travel, and the lessons from his first five years of early retirement. There's also a post on Mrs. Root of Good's early retirement.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **Start on day one.** Saving from your very first paycheck is far easier than cutting back later.
- **You don't need a high salary.** A 50% savings rate on an ordinary income still gets you there.
- **Plan for your spending to change.** Kids grow, and so do costs. Leave some room in your number.

## What it would look like in Denmark

Their budget of about $40,000 a year is roughly **260,000 kr.** for a family of five. That gives an FI number of about **6.5 million kr.** with the 4% rule.

A Danish family would likely need less. Healthcare and education, including university, are free, and the *børne- og ungeydelse* (child benefit) helps with costs while the children are young. The flip side: much of a typical Danish household's savings sits in pension accounts that can't be touched until a few years before state pension age. Anyone retiring early in Denmark needs enough in freely available savings, such as an *aktiesparekonto* and a normal investment account, to bridge the gap.
