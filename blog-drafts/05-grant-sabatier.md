# From $2.26 in the bank to a millionaire in five years: the side-hustle route to FI

> **In short:** In 2010, aged 24, Grant Sabatier woke up with $2.26 in his bank account. Five years later his net worth was over $1.25 million, and he was financially independent at 30. He didn't get there by cutting coupons. He focused on earning more and investing almost all of it.

## The starting point

Rock bottom. Sabatier has told the story many times: in 2010, at 24, he checked his bank account and found $2.26. No savings, no plan.

## The turning point

He set himself a very specific goal: **$1 million in five years.** When he did the math, he saw that saving from a normal salary alone would take far too long. So he looked at the side of the equation most money advice skips: **income.**

## The plan

- **Earn more, in as many ways as possible.** He built several streams of side income. Some of it was a bit unusual, like cat sitting and buying and reselling VW camper vans.
- **Invest the extra.** The new income went into investments instead of a bigger lifestyle.
- **Track progress against the goal.** A clear number and a deadline kept him focused.

## The hard parts

Getting there that fast took real sacrifice, and he has talked openly in interviews about what he gave up to do it. This route trades five very intense years for decades of freedom, and that trade isn't for everyone.

## Life after FI

He founded the website *Millennial Money* and wrote *Financial Freedom*, and he has kept building businesses because he enjoys it, not because he has to.

## Lessons worth stealing

- **Income has no ceiling. Cutting costs does.** You can only cut spending to zero, but there's no limit to how much you can earn.
- **Set a specific number and a deadline.** "$1 million in 5 years" is a plan. "Save more" is a wish.
- **Small side hustles add up.** Cat sitting won't make you rich on its own, but every extra 1,000 invested early grows for decades.

## What it would look like in Denmark

$1.25 million is about **8 million kr.**, which with the 4% rule would pay about **27,000 kr. a month**.

The income route works differently in Denmark. Extra income from side work is taxed as personal income, and the top tax rate means a big share of each extra krone goes to tax. On the other hand, wages are high and there's a strong safety net, so taking a career risk costs less here. A Danish version might put more weight on career moves and pay rises than on side hustles. The principle stays the same: grow the gap between what you earn and what you spend, and invest it.
